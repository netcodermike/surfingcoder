How Dropped Scores Reshape the Race for the World Title

A question for Pipeline

Should Gabriel Medina and Carissa Moore be wearing the gold jerseys at Pipeline?

Not under the published rankings—Yago Dora and Molly Picklum still lead. But once you account for the scores each surfer can discard, Medina and Moore have a stronger same-finish position. That is the argument behind the question, rather than a claim that the official jerseys have been awarded incorrectly.

Pipeline has plenty riding on it already. Dropped scores add another twist: Medina and Moore can catch the leaders without finishing ahead of them.

Same finish. A different leader.

Picture Dora and Medina both losing their opening heat at Pipeline. A frustrating finish for both—and a change at the top of their points battle. Yago Dora arrives with a 1,740-point lead, yet Gabriel Medina would finish the season ahead of him. The women’s race has the same twist: Carissa Moore overtakes Molly Picklum if both finish ninth.

That is the wrinkle to keep in mind while watching Pipe. The published rankings tell us who leads today. To see what happens next, we also need to know which old result each surfer gets to throw away. The same finish at Pipeline can mean very different things to the surfers chasing a world title.

That alone would not hand Medina or Moore a world title; another contender could still pass them. It does show why the race deserves a closer look. A 15,000-point event sounds simple enough, until you see how much of that score actually counts.

Scope: a complete 12-event season, normal competitive participation, and the published advanced seeding. Withdrawals, disciplinary changes, or a revised event format would require a new calculation. The tables below are points conditions applied to feasible results, not heat-by-heat predictions.

What a Pipe result really buys you

Here is the rule that makes it click. The 2026 WSL rulebook counts a surfer’s best nine results from twelve events. Right now, the rankings count nine of the eleven completed events. Add Pipeline, drop the three lowest scores, and keep the remaining nine. A good result earns its place by pushing an older score out.

Final points = current points + max(0, Pipe points − replacement score)The replacement score is the lowest of the nine results currently counting. If Pipe is lower, Pipe itself is discarded.

Put Dora and Medina through that calculation. Dora’s score to replace is 3,320; Medina’s is just 1,000. A men’s ninth at Pipe pays 4,980. Dora gains 1,660, while Medina gains 3,980. Same finish, but a 2,320-point swing toward Medina—enough to wipe out Dora’s lead.

For Picklum and Moore, the contrast is even easier to see. Picklum replaces 4,745, while Moore replaces 2,000. A women’s ninth is worth 3,000, so Picklum throws that Pipe result away and stays on 53,695. Moore gets a net 1,000-point lift to 54,150. That is how a matching result can flip their order.

Dora, Medina, Ferreira, Colapinto, Pupo and Fioravanti are still in it. For Dora and Medina, the clearest instruction is the same: win Pipe and the world title is yours. Beyond that, their safety nets look different. Medina can still become champion with ninth if the other results fall his way. Dora needs at least fifth.

Where Ewing’s chase runs out

Ewing could win the event and still come up short in the title race. He starts seventh on 41,725 and replaces 1,000, so a Pipe win takes him to 55,725. The top eight men start in Round 4, with ninth their worst normal finish. Even there, Medina reaches 57,190. Ewing cannot catch that floor, and everyone below him is also out under these assumptions.

A tour place can turn on 50 points

The trophy is only part of the story at Pipe. Farther down the rankings, surfers are fighting for a place on next season’s tour: the top 22 men and top 14 women qualify. The drops matter here just as much. Missing the postseason does not make all of a surfer’s Pipe points a fresh gain; a new counting score still pushes their lowest counting result out.

There is some breathing room for the current top 15 men and top nine women. Our conservative calculation guarantees their CT requalification through normal competition, even when we assign the available finishing places against them. The final draw may protect more surfers. Falling outside these guaranteed groups does not, by itself, mean a surfer can actually be knocked out.

Hopkins, Gilmore, and the 50-point difference

Hopkins gives us a particularly tense example. Lose her first heat and finish 17th, and Yolanda Hopkins ends on 27,075. All current top 13 women are certain to finish ahead. That leaves her watching the chase from below: she keeps the remaining qualifying spot only if nobody behind her passes that total.

A Gilmore semifinal would do it. Stephanie Gilmore finishing third produces 27,125—just 50 points more than Hopkins. A semifinal for Anat Lelior or Alyssa Spencer also gets past her; Brisa Hennessy, Tya Zebrowski and Vahine Fierro need the final, while Sally Fitzgibbons and Bella Kenworthy need wins. Each of those results would displace a Hopkins who exits in the first round.

Hopkins can also take the waiting out of it. Third gets her to 34,700, beyond every current outsider’s maximum, and guarantees her place. A semifinal is a clear target with a clear reward: she would no longer need the results behind her to cooperate.

Marshall and Mamiya: level on points, down to the details

Marshall and Mamiya have an especially close watch ahead. Jake Marshall and Barron Mamiya both start on 19,705 and replace 1,000, giving them identical final-points schedules. If both finish 25th, they each reach 20,205 with the current top 21 men still ahead. A challenger passing that score pushes them below the line. If nobody does, their tie sends us to the qualification procedure.

Even their regular-season result distributions are identical, so the current ranking order does not settle it. The rulebook’s countback, average-heat-score step and possible accommodation through an available WSL wildcard all matter. Challenger Series qualification and discretionary wildcard decisions remain separate routes back to the tour.

For Oscar Berry and Francisca Veselko, Pipe points alone cannot get them to their CT cutoffs. Several other low-ranked surfers still have points-based paths. Whether they can actually take those paths depends on entry eligibility and the final draw.

Check the work

If you want to dig into a favorite surfer’s chances, the full work is below. We used the individual event points for all 35 ranked men and 23 ranked women after the Philippines Pro. Every published total matches the best nine of eleven. We then added each possible Pipe result and recalculated the best nine. On November 9, 2026, we checked all 58 ranking rows again against the live WSL tables; they still matched this November 3 snapshot. We independently recalculated every projected finish, all 33 listed title conditions, and the stored conservative qualification bounds. The numerical conclusions remain unchanged.

For requalification, the calculation respects the number of places available in each finishing band and the normal entry-round floors. Those aggregate bounds do not model every matchup. The men’s provisional seed template retrieved for the analysis contains a duplicate seed, so this article avoids presenting it as a settled named draw.

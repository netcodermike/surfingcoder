Pipeline Title Scenarios.

Mapping the possibilities, the pressure, and the paths to a title at Pipeline.

FIELD NOTES / 002
One wave. Many possibilities.
Sample article: this is a fictional season with an additive points system and Pipeline as its final event. It does not describe the current WSL championship format or an actual title race.

A final event is easier to follow when you know what each result changes. “Needs a big finish” sounds dramatic, but it is not a scenario. A useful scenario tells you which finish is enough, whose result matters, and what happens in a tie.

Start with explicit assumptions

For this example, only two surfers remain in title contention. A leads with 30,000 points and B has 28,000. All event points are added directly; no results are discarded. Our fictional final event awards 5,000 for a win, 3,000 for runner-up, and 1,000 for an earlier exit.

These simplified rules let us focus on the method. In a real analysis, the first job would be to verify the event format, points schedule, counting rules, and tiebreak procedure for the season in question.

A cannot lose the points lead by winning. B can move ahead outright by winning if A exits early. If B wins and A is runner-up, the totals tie; the points alone do not identify the champion.

Possible is not probable

A scenario matrix is a map, not a forecast. It does not tell us how likely each result is. Giving every cell equal weight would be an assumption, not a neutral calculation.

A credible probability model would need more: draw constraints, opponent strength, conditions, and enough historical evidence to test the model. Even then, uncertainty belongs in the presentation.

Make the live story simpler

As the event progresses, remove outcomes that are no longer possible. Once a contender is eliminated, freeze that result and update the remaining paths. The full matrix becomes a short list of meaningful conditions.

The best output is not necessarily a complicated chart. It might be one clear sentence: “B needs a win and an early exit from A to lead outright.” Behind that sentence, though, there should be a calculation someone else can reproduce.
